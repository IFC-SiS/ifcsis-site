C-SiS · Descriptor note

Images

Describe the anatomy, not the garment

4 November 2026 · S. Harrison Knudson, J.D.

An image is described by what it shows of the human body, in terms every country's doctors already share. Nothing in the description says whether the image is acceptable. That is for the curator and the parent.

The anatomical vocabulary

Descriptors come from internationally recognized anatomical terminology, maintained by anatomists, with Latin as the anchor and agreed equivalents in national languages. Each descriptor therefore already exists in every language a doctor works in. “Nudity” is too large a word to be useful; C-SiS breaks it into anatomical regions, including scalp hair.

Coverage states

Each region carries one observable state: uncovered; sheer (skin visible through fabric); covered, contour visible (tight or clinging); covered, contour shaped (lifted or padded); covered, contour concealed (opaque and loose). Two people looking at the same image would assign the same values without sharing any beliefs.

Describe the anatomy, not the garment

Garments are never named in the standard. A bare midriff is the abdominal region uncovered; a bikini and a thong differ only in coverage of the gluteal region; a headcover is defined by whether it covers scalp hair, neck or face. Communities and curators may publish their own garment definitions in these terms. Whether uncovered hair matters is the curator's call, but the descriptor exists for everyone.

Figures and depictions

Stick figures, avatars, emoji and robots with faces or limbs count as people; there is no exemption for stylization. Implied nudity counts. Staged, produced work is distinguished from found footage.

Declared at entry

Some facts, such as padding, cannot reliably be read from an image. The publisher declares the descriptors when content enters, as a representation it is answerable for, rather than leaving a system to guess afterward.

The body is described. The meaning belongs to the people who hold it.

This note may be read and circulated freely. Critical reactions — terminological, technical or practical — are more useful than supportive ones.
