WP-05 · Working paper

The Geography of Zero

Interacting frictions and institutional design after distance

First revision · September 2026

Distance never acted alone

The internet is commonly said to have overcome distance. That is true but incomplete. Physical distance generated and reinforced a bundle of frictions that operated together: travel, attendance, search, matching, coordination, translation, temporal access, and the social visibility of being somewhere in person.

Networked technologies reduce those frictions. What matters is not that they all fall at once, but that they interact. Removing one friction increases the significance of removing another. A distant lecture becomes more accessible when travel is unnecessary; more so when it can be watched asynchronously, found by search, and made intelligible by machine translation; and different again when it can be consumed privately, without visible attendance.

The resulting condition is what this paper calls the Geography of Zero. Zero is functional rather than literal. It does not mean costlessness, and it does not mean geography has disappeared. It describes a threshold at which interacting frictions fall low enough that they cease reliably to perform their former sorting or boundary-maintenance roles. Geography remains. An increasing class of human activity no longer requires its traversal.

The question economics does not ask

This builds on established scholarship rather than displacing it. Coasean and Northian institutional economics explains how institutions form under positive transaction costs and change when those costs change. Meyrowitz, Castells, Harvey and Giddens explain how electronic media loosen the tie between social activity and physical place.

Economics asks what friction costs us. This framework also asks what friction was doing.

That second question does not presume the consequences of friction were desirable, and it creates no presumption in favour of preservation. Some consequences of friction should be allowed to disappear. Some will be replaced spontaneously. Some will remain contested. The question is which is which, and the paper's contribution is a method for telling them apart.

Three tests

Three tests identify a friction-dependent function. Each is a gate at which an application of the theory can fail.

Dependency. Did the disputed outcome materially depend on the friction, or did it merely coexist with it? Correlation across a period when the friction was present is not sufficient.

Independent demand. After the friction declines, do people or institutions still demand the outcome? The strongest evidence is revealed reconstruction: actors incurring new costs to reproduce an outcome after the friction that once helped supply it has substantially declined. Willingness to pay for a substitute is a harder test than expressed preference.

Separability. If demand persists, can the outcome be supplied by another mechanism without recreating the underlying cost? An outcome that can only be restored by restoring the friction has failed this test, whatever its value.

Only outcomes passing all three reach the problem of architectural substitution: relocating a valued function to another institutional or technical layer rather than restoring an obsolete cost.

Child online safety as an instance

Physical societies differentiate children's environments from adults' through buildings, distance, supervision, law, admission rules and norms. Networked access weakens the physical-separation component of that without eliminating apparent demand for differentiated environments. Parental controls, managed school systems, child accounts, age-differentiated platform experiences and government assurance mechanisms are all attempts to reproduce something that separation used to supply.

That pattern is evidence of revealed reconstruction. It is not proof that any particular intervention is desirable, and the paper does not treat it as such.

The admission-layer architecture this foundation works on is one candidate case of architectural substitution: an attempt to reproduce selected environmental and admission boundaries at the namespace and governance layers while retaining low-cost global access. The theory does not entail that architecture. It requires comparing it against device, application, identity and legal alternatives, and it supplies the tests under which the comparison could go against it.

Why this paper sits beneath the others

The rest of this series argues from a particular place — that the domain address behaves like land, that a burden was misallocated to households, that demand has been measured with the wrong instrument. Each of those assumes something this paper tries to make explicit: that the loss of distance removed a set of functions nobody had to specify while distance was still supplying them, and that specifying them is a precondition for arguing about how to replace them.

The research programme runs in sequence: interacting friction decline, then friction-dependent outcome, then the dependency test, then revealed reconstruction, then separability, then architectural substitution, then comparative institutional analysis. Nothing in that chain is settled by this paper. The objective is not to put the mountains back. It is to identify which outcomes remain independently demanded after the rough places became plain, and to determine whether those can be supplied at another institutional layer without requiring anyone to climb the mountain again.

What would falsify it

The framework fails if the three tests cannot distinguish obsolete adaptation from independently valued function in practice — if, applied to real cases, they admit everything or exclude everything. It fails for any given application if the outcome turns out not to have depended on the friction, if demand does not survive the friction's decline, or if no substitute exists that does not reimpose the cost. Applied to child online safety specifically, it fails if the reconstruction behaviour now observable is better explained as habit, marketing or regulatory compliance than as demand for something separation used to provide.
