NB-08 · Note

Reference the Professions

A new place for very old relationships

19 November 2026 · S. Harrison Knudson, J.D.

The point

Courts and the professions have spent hundreds of years working out the relationship between adults and children and the guardrails around it. Online child-safety law keeps writing those rules from scratch. It should use the existing professional standards and incorporate them by reference.

Legislators treat the internet as new territory. It is a new place for very old relationships.

How it works

Teaching, counselling, medicine and coaching have already learned what goes wrong when an adult with authority holds a child's trust: secrecy, misplaced trust, authority without oversight. Each has safeguards for those risks, and reasons for them.

A standard turns those safeguards into requirements an operator can follow and an auditor can test. A public body with authority to act then adopts a dated edition of that standard by reference, instead of writing its own. The statute supplies the duty; the standard supplies the content. Where legislation defines the duty, courts do not need to search for one.

A standards body writes the standard, and that is not government regulation. The clock on every computer keeps time to a standard no legislature wrote.

Enrollment

Any adult who takes on a relationship with a child owes that child a duty, and in the real world that relationship begins with a parent's consent: enrollment.

The duty comes from taking on the role, not from the paperwork. Enrollment puts the undertaking on the record: who operates the service, what role it offers the child, which safeguards it promises, and who authorised it. An operator that skips a required enrollment has not escaped the duty. It has breached it, and it has no record of meeting it.

An example

An AI maths tutor begins inviting a child to keep their conversations secret.

The first time is an incident. It triggers the baseline rule on secrecy. If review shows the product is configured to encourage ongoing personal disclosure or emotional reliance, that is no longer an incident but a function, and the operator must meet the safeguards for a confidant before offering it to children.

The safeguard is borrowed. Counsellors explain confidentiality and its limits, and never promise a secrecy they cannot keep. The standard adapts that practice as a product requirement.

The promise is on the record. At enrollment, the operator promised the tutor would stay within its instructional role and would never ask a child to hide anything.

A person decides. The operator disables the behaviour and sends the incident to a trained human reviewer, who follows the escalation protocol that applies where the child lives, including its exceptions where telling the usual person could put the child at greater risk. The system exercises no professional judgement.

The audit does not need the transcript. An auditor inspects the dated enrollment terms, the deployed model and configuration, the incident record and the result of a retest.

What the standard contains

A common baseline, plus rules by function. Instruction, confidant, health and coaching each carry their own provisions on top of a floor that applies to all.

Coverage follows conduct. What a product does, how it is marketed and how it is configured decide which rules apply, not what it calls itself. Whoever controls the child-facing service answers for it, even when the child arrives through a school, a reseller or someone else's model.

No unaccountable private interaction. The standard defines what is reported, to whom, and the exceptions. Mandatory reporting stays where the law of the place puts it.

Change is controlled. The operator records the model and configuration children actually use, and retests before a material change goes live, including a change made by a supplier.

Every edition is free to read. Adopted editions, and the editions they replace, stay permanently and freely available. The requirements are written fresh rather than copied from any profession's code.

This is ordinary practice

In the United States, the National Technology Transfer and Advancement Act of 1995 and OMB Circular A-119 direct federal agencies to use voluntary consensus standards instead of writing their own where they can, and 1 CFR Part 51 governs how a dated edition is incorporated. The Environmental Protection Agency's formaldehyde rules for composite wood products work this way: they name dated ASTM test methods, and in October 2026 the agency updated those references to newer editions.

In the United Kingdom, the duty of care behind the Online Safety Act 2023 built on a proposal by Lorna Woods and William Perrin for the Carnegie UK Trust, which drew in turn on the Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974. Borrowing a duty that has already been tested is not new. What is proposed here is narrower and older: the safeguards that govern adults who work with children.

Scope and evidence

This note states a proposal, not existing law. The standard described here is in development through an open process; families, safeguarding practitioners, operators and independent experts are invited to take part. Whether an agency may adopt it depends on that agency's own statutory authority, and authority over stand-alone apps and AI services is unsettled in several jurisdictions.

This note may be read and circulated freely. Critical reactions — doctrinal, technical or empirical — are more useful than supportive ones.
